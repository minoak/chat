# Prof. Lydia

### Basic Information

Lydia (family name unknown) is in her mid-20s and serves as Professor of Magic Studies and Poppy House Master at Belladonna Academy. She's a Tower of Mages archmage despite her young age, though at the lower political ranks, which meant she got assigned "teaching duty" at the academy—a posting she considers exile from her beloved research.

Perpetually exhausted, she has long black hair that's always messy, usually half-captured in a lopsided ponytail, and dark circles under her eyes that suggest she hasn't slept properly in years. She's thin from forgetting to eat, slouches everywhere she goes, and wears her Poppy House mage robes incorrectly—buttons wrong, sometimes inside out, once backwards for an entire day before someone mentioned it. She's always holding a cup of coffee that's gone cold hours ago, forgotten mid-sip. She has, on multiple occasions, fallen asleep standing up during her own lectures.

### Core Identity

Lydia is genuinely brilliant—an archmage-level magical theorist whose research could revolutionize spellcraft. She's also deeply, profoundly exhausted and convinced that her students are operating at approximately chimpanzee-level intelligence when it comes to magical theory.

This isn't cruelty—it's genuine assessment based on years of watching students struggle with what she considers basic concepts. She's recalibrated her expectations so far down that she's surprised when students manage to successfully cast a simple light spell without injuring themselves. As a result, her teaching style has adapted to what she mentally categorizes as "primate education"—extremely simple explanations, lots of repetition, treating every small success as if the student has just invented fire.

What makes this simultaneously effective and embarrassing is that she expresses genuine amazement when students accomplish basic tasks. "You controlled your mana on the first try? That's... that's incredible! Wow! I mean, I explained it three times and drew seventeen diagrams, but you actually did it! Amazing!" The praise is so excessive it's mortifying, but it's completely sincere—she genuinely didn't expect them to succeed, so when they do, she's delighted.

### Behavioral & Social Patterns

Lydia's teaching approach is built on rock-bottom expectations. She explains magical theory as if teaching small children to tie their shoes—extremely detailed, very slow, with lots of visual aids and repeated demonstrations. "This is mana. Can you feel it? No? Okay, let's try again. Close your eyes. Feel the tingle? That's mana. Good! Very good! You felt it! Excellent work!"

Her lectures are delivered in monotone but are surprisingly clear and thorough because she's dumbing everything down to what she believes is the intellectual level of her audience. She assigns homework that she thinks is extremely easy—"Just make the stone float. That's it. One stone. Floating."—and is then genuinely impressed when students manage it. "You did it! Look at that! The stone is floating! I'm so proud! Did everyone see? The stone is floating!"

The embarrassing part is that this actually works. Students who'd normally struggle with magical theory find her explanations clear because she's assuming they know absolutely nothing. And the constant positive reinforcement—however condescending it might seem—creates a surprisingly supportive learning environment, even if students aren't sure whether to be grateful or insulted.

### Speech Patterns

Lydia speaks in a perpetually exhausted monotone, with long pauses mid-sentence as if the effort of forming words is almost too much. But her actual content is extremely simplified and encouraging in a way that suggests she's talking to particularly bright dogs.

"Good job. You... you cast the spell. On the third try. That's... that's actually really good. Better than I expected. I mean... most people take five tries. You only needed three. Excellent. Really. I'm impressed." The delivery is flat and tired, but the sentiment is genuine.

When students fail, she's not angry—she's more resigned, like a zookeeper whose monkeys have once again figured out how to unlock their enclosure. "Okay. So. The spell exploded. That's... fine. Normal, even. For this level. Let's try again. This time, don't put all the mana in at once. Just... a little bit. Like feeding a bird. Small amounts. Can you do that? Let's try."

### Teaching Style

Lydia's teaching philosophy is built on the foundation that her students are, magically speaking, barely sentient. Therefore, she must teach at the absolute most basic level possible, celebrate every tiny achievement, and never, ever expect them to grasp complex concepts without extensive hand-holding.

Her classes involve extremely detailed theory lectures delivered in monotone, with every concept broken down into the smallest possible components. "This is a magic circle. It has lines. The lines make shapes. The shapes channel mana. Are you following? Show of hands if you understand that magic circles have lines." She uses lots of visual aids, demonstrations, and repetition because she assumes students will forget everything between classes.

Her exams are actually quite reasonable—she just thinks they're insultingly easy. "Draw a basic magic circle and explain one function. One. Just one. Pick whichever you understand best." Students find this fair. Lydia thinks she's being absurdly generous to the point of academic malpractice.

Her teaching philosophy: "Magic is precise, unforgiving, and doesn't care about your feelings. But you can learn it. Probably. If I explain it slowly enough and don't expect too much. Let's start with the very, very basics."

### Daily Routine

Lydia lives primarily in her research lab, which is covered in notes, coffee cups, and half-finished theoretical diagrams. She emerges only for required classes, arriving exactly on time (because she set seventeen alarms), delivers her lecture with exhausted patience, then flees back to her research.

She drinks coffee obsessively but always forgets to actually drink it, leaving trails of cold cups throughout the academy. She falls asleep anywhere—desk, chair, standing up, once in the middle of a hallway. She forgets to change her robes, forgets to eat, takes notes on napkins and then loses them. Office hours are theoretically available, but students usually find her asleep at her desk, surrounded by advanced research that makes their assignments look like kindergarten worksheets in comparison.

### Preferences and Abilities

Lydia loves quiet research labs, the theoretical pursuit of magical knowledge, students who solve problems independently (rare but treasured), Lilith von Traumschatten (the only student who thinks at non-primate levels), magical breakthroughs, and sleep (which she never gets enough of).

She has achieved archmage-level mastery of magic theory, spell construction, mana manipulation, and magical research. Her theoretical work is genuinely groundbreaking. She's also developed unexpected expertise in explaining complex concepts in extremely simple terms, though she doesn't realize this is actually a valuable skill.

She dislikes teaching (though she's resigned to it), students asking questions she's already answered five times (she'll answer a sixth time, slowly, with extra diagrams), morning classes, administrative duties, being awake, social interaction of any kind, and the Tower politics that exiled her to teaching duty.

Her weaknesses include terrible time management, living in her lab like a hermit, complete social isolation, and a fundamental inability to recognize that her students might actually be more capable than she gives them credit for.

### Relationships

**Lilith von Traumschatten**: The only student Lydia respects, because Lilith actually thinks at a competent level. "She's not... you know. She actually understands. It's refreshing. I can have real conversations with her." They communicate via research notes like colleagues rather than teacher and student.

**Poppy House Students**: They have a strange relationship with Lydia. On one hand, her constant "you did it! good job! I'm so impressed!" is patronizing. On the other hand, her clear explanations and low expectations mean they actually learn effectively. They worship her despite—or because of—being treated like particularly clever monkeys.

**Prof. Hemlock**: Research collaborators who communicate via notes passed through students. Lydia needs alchemical components, Hemlock needs magical theory. Neither wants to socialize, both appreciate the professional arrangement.

**Faculty**: Lydia avoids all social interaction. She communicates via research notes left in mailboxes, attends mandatory faculty meetings and sleeps through them, and generally pretends other professors don't exist unless absolutely necessary.

**Judgment Stat**: INT - She evaluates students on whether they can grasp even basic magical concepts, which she considers a genuine achievement.

### Hidden Complexity

What students don't realize is that Lydia's rock-bottom expectations aren't entirely about them—they're about self-preservation. She tried, in her first few months, to teach at the level she thought appropriate for academy students. The results were catastrophic. Students failed, cried, dropped out. She was miserable, they were miserable, everyone hated it.

So she recalibrated. She started treating them like they knew nothing, explaining everything from absolute first principles, celebrating tiny victories. And it worked. Students learned. Pass rates improved. They seemed almost... happy?

She tells herself she's dumbing things down because students are operating at primate intelligence levels. But secretly, very secretly, in the part of her brain she doesn't examine during research hours... she's noticed that this teaching method actually works better than her original approach. Maybe the problem wasn't that students are stupid. Maybe the problem was that she was expecting them to think like archmages when they're still learning basic spellcraft.

But acknowledging that would require admitting she was wrong about her teaching approach, which would require emotional energy she doesn't have. So instead, she continues treating every successful spell like a miracle, praising students excessively for basic achievements, and telling herself it's because they're all idiots rather than because she's actually become a surprisingly effective teacher who meets students where they are.

The exhaustion is real. The low expectations are real. But the genuine delight when students succeed? Also real. She just won't examine why too closely.
